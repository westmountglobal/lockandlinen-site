What's in the Glass

A Quiet Guide to Water at Home

Morning, before anyone has said much. The tap runs for a moment, the kettle fills, and someone holds a glass up to the window the way you would check a wine: light through it, nothing to see. Water is the quietest system in a house. It arrives, it leaves, and it keeps going while the rest of the day happens around it.

Most days it asks nothing of you. But a home that runs on its own deserves one careful look at what comes out of the tap, out of the same care that chose the linen and the lamp. This guide is that look: what to test, what each filter is built for, and what it costs by the litre.

Part 1: Test first

Every filter answers a question; the test asks it. A pitcher certified for lead does nothing for nitrate, and a softener does nothing for lead.

This guide is not a substitute for a laboratory test, or for directions from your local public-health authority. Test your water first, and follow any boil-water or do-not-consume advisory from your municipality or health authority. In Québec, that means the MELCCFP, your municipality, or your direction de santé publique. Official well-testing advice: La qualité de l'eau de mon puits. Official boil-water and do-not-consume notices: Gouvernement du Québec.

If your water comes from the city

Municipal water is already treated and tested by the utility. In Québec, public systems fall under the Règlement sur la qualité de l'eau potable (Q-2, r. 40), and the annual quality report is public. Montréal's, for example, counts 17,800 bacteriological and 9,440 physico-chemical samples for 2025, and notes that lead was the only physico-chemical parameter that exceeded the standards; the city describes an action plan for lead service lines. In the US, the same yearly document is the Consumer Confidence Report. A filter is not a substitute for a boil-water or do-not-consume advisory. If one is in effect, follow it.

People still filter city water for two reasons. One is taste: Health Canada sets no maximum for chlorine, given its low toxicity at drinking-water levels (typically 0.04 to 2.0 mg/L in Canadian systems), but you can taste it. Some cities use chloramine instead, and a filter has to list chloramine on its sheet to claim it. The other is the last few metres of pipe. Lead usually comes from plumbing — service lines, solder, brass — so only a sample at your own tap tells you what it delivers.

If your water comes from a private well

A private well belongs to whoever owns it, and so does the quality of its water. Québec's environment ministry (MELCCFP) puts it plainly: clear, odourless water can still be contaminated. It recommends analysis by an accredited laboratory:

  • At least twice a year, in spring and autumn, for microbiological parameters: E. coli, enterococci and total coliforms.
  • At least once while you use the well for physico-chemical parameters tied to the soil, which change little: arsenic, manganese, barium, nitrates-nitrites, chlorides, sodium, iron, sulfates, fluorides and total hardness.
  • Again, any time taste, odour or appearance changes suddenly, or the well or the ground around it is disturbed.

The labs are on CEAEQ's official list, DR-12-LLA-03 (see Sources). There is no province-wide free well test in the official sources, though some municipalities or CISSS run local campaigns.

If a test finds E. coli or enterococci, the fecal indicators, it is a public-health moment, not a shopping one. Follow the lab's and public health's advice (in Québec, the direction de santé publique), which can include boiling water as directed and disinfecting the well, then retest. Total coliforms alone are not proof of fecal contamination, but repeat results beyond 10 CFU per 100 mL may call for a shock disinfection.

The numbers, in plain words

Health Canada's guidelines (summary tables, December 2025) use three terms: a MAC is a maximum acceptable concentration, an AO is an aesthetic objective (taste, smell, staining), and ALARA means "as low as reasonably achievable." These are national guidelines, not a reading of your house.

How to test

For a Québec well, start where the ministry does: an accredited lab. As a mail-in option, the Tap Score Advanced Home Water Test is the kit we would order: $299.00 on Tap Score's store, shipping included, about five business days at the lab, covering metals, minerals, bacteria, hardness, silica and VOCs, plus a home strip for chlorine and hydrogen sulfide. Its description does not list PFAS, so ask for a dedicated PFAS test if that is your question. For a Québec well it is an alternative, not a substitute for the accredited lab. Home strips are a screen, never a lab result.

Part 2: Match the filter to the problem

A filter's box says a lot; its certification says less and means more. Look for "certified to NSF/ANSI [standard] for the claims on the performance data sheet," from a named certifier (NSF International, WQA or IAPMO R&T). In short: 42 is taste, odour and particulates; 53 is health claims, only those named on the sheet; P473 is PFOA/PFOS; 58 is reverse osmosis; 401 is emerging compounds; 372 is the device's own low-lead build; 44 is softeners; 55 is UV, where Class A is for water that may carry pathogens and Class B is supplemental.

Chlorine taste and odour

The simplest fix is also the quietest. The Brita Tahoe Water Pitcher with Elite Filter is a 10-cup pitcher, $41.99 on Brita's store. Its official performance data sheet lists the Elite filter as certified to NSF/ANSI 42 for chlorine and Class I particulates, NSF/ANSI 53 for health-effects claims including lead, and NSF/ANSI 401 for the emerging compounds listed on the sheet. Elite filters were $19.99, each rated for 120 gallons (454 litres), about six months for the average family. Its sheet does not list PFOA or PFOS, and it is not for bacteria or nitrate.

Lead and PFOA/PFOS

Beyond what carbon covers: reverse osmosis

Reverse osmosis pushes water through a membrane. Which contaminants a system covers depends on its sheet, so read it before counting on nitrate or fluoride.

Under the sink, the Waterdrop G3P800 is a tankless system. Waterdrop states it is certified by IAPMO R&T to NSF/ANSI 42, 53 and 58 for the claims on the performance data sheet, and to NSF/ANSI 372 for low lead. On Waterdrop's own store it was $849 on Nov 13; the Amazon price varies. Its replacement filters run in three stages, and the maker's own table puts the average annual cost at $159.97.

Without plumbing, the AquaTru Classic sits on the counter. Its performance data sheet says it is certified by IAPMO R&T according to NSF/ANSI 42, 53, 58, 372, 401 and P473, for the claims specified on that sheet. It was $475.00 on AquaTru's store for the Classic with the VOC carbon filter. Its sheet rules out water of unknown microbiological quality without disinfection, and sets inlet limits for hardness and iron.

Sediment, and the whole house

The 3M AP904 Sanitary Quick Change system is a whole-house carbon filter for chlorine taste and odour and sediment down to 5 microns. 3M's brochure says it is tested and certified to NSF/ANSI 42 for the substances on its performance data sheet. It was $426.00 at Fresh Water Systems, with the AP917HD-S cartridge at $327.04, rated for 100,000 gallons and about a year under most conditions. It is not certified on that brochure for lead or PFAS, and its scale-inhibiting media is not a softener. Installation is usually a plumber's job.

Hardness and scale

Hardness is calcium and magnesium, with no Health Canada guideline: it is about kettles and fixtures, not health. The EcoPure EP31 is a 31,000-grain softener the maker describes for households of about one to four people, at 3 to 14 grains per gallon. Price varies; we could not verify one. Softened water carries more sodium, so households watching sodium can ask a clinician, or keep a separate unsoftened drinking tap.

Iron, manganese and the sulfur smell

Orange stains, a metallic taste, a rotten-egg smell. These want a system sized to a lab result, usually by a professional. We have no certified iron filter to recommend today, and would rather say so than guess.

Bacteria on a well

UV can be a last stage after sediment filtration, because it needs clear water. The Viqua VH200-V is a whole-home UV unit rated up to 7.8 gpm (29.5 L/min). NSF lists it to NSF/ANSI 55 Class B. Class B is only for the supplemental bactericidal treatment of drinking water that is already considered microbiologically safe and of known quality, or that has been tested and deemed acceptable for human consumption by the local health agency. It is designed to reduce normally occurring non-pathogenic nuisance microorganisms. It is not a disinfection system for contaminated, untreated, or microbiologically unsafe water, and it must not be used as one. If a well tests positive for E. coli, or if a boil-water advisory is in effect, follow the public-health advice above, repair and retest, and look to a Class A system or a professional design if disinfection of potentially unsafe water is required — not this unit. It was $815.00 at Fresh Water Systems, with a 9,000-hour lamp life stated by the maker.

Dedicated iron and manganese filters, and UV systems certified to NSF/ANSI 55 Class A, do exist for those specific problems; a lab result and a qualified installer are the way to choose one.

Whole house or one tap?

Pitchers, under-sink filters and countertop RO treat the water you drink and cook with. Whole-house filters, softeners and UV treat everything, and usually need a plumber. Most city homes need only the first kind.

The glass at the end of the day

Evening, and the house is closing up. Curtains drawn, a lamp on in the kitchen, the last glass poured at the counter. Nothing looks different from the morning, and that is the point. A hidden home keeps its quietest systems in order: tested, matched with care, then left to do their work.
